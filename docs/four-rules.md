# The YNAB Four Rules, distilled

An operational distillation of Mecham's framework with chapter pointers, written so a
skill can cite a rule and move on instead of re-explaining it every session.
`four-rules-index.md` is the one-page version. `chapter-map.md` maps the citations to
the book's structure.

**This is summary and commentary, not the book.** It skips the stories, the worked
examples, and the reasoning that makes the rules land the first time. If the framework
is new to you, buy the book. This file is for someone who has already read it and needs
the rules in a form a machine can reference.

**A note on the couples material.** Chapter 6 recommends a Yours/Mine/Ours split where
each partner keeps a small unaccountable "fun money" category. Some households prefer
one shared pot instead, with every category a joint decision. Both models work. Pick
one, set it as `MONEY_MODEL` in the `ynab-coach` config, and do not let the assistant
relitigate it every month.

## The Foundational Question

> *What do I want my money to do for me?*, Intro

This replaces the two questions that drive most money stress:
- *Can I afford this?* (paralysis from finite resources)
- *Should I do this?* (paralysis from social comparison)

**The mindset in one sentence (Ch 1):** *"Forget future money; use today's money to write your future."*

You only budget money you have **right now**. Forecasting (assigning dollars to a future paycheck) is dreaming, not budgeting.

**Embrace scarcity (Ch 1).** Money is finite. The feeling of scarcity is a feature, it forces intentional choices.

**The new filter for spending decisions:** *Does this move me closer to my goal?* (replaces *Can I afford this?*).

## Rule Zero: Decide What Matters (Ch 6)

Mecham introduces this in the couples chapter, but it applies to everyone. Before Rule One can work, you must decide what's most important. Without that, "give every dollar a job" is just busywork.

**Method:** List priorities, including ambitious ones with no dollars assigned yet. Mecham keeps an unfunded "family cabin" line in his budget so he never forgets. Add ambitious notes to the budget *before* dollars are available, that placeholder makes the goal real.

**In a partnership:** this is SHARED priority discovery. Both partners surface what each values, what they value together, and what life they are designing. The Monthly Budget Date (Ch 6) is the venue. Rule Zero is what makes Rules 1 to 4 work at all; without it the rules are busywork.

## Rule One: Give Every Dollar a Job (Ch 2)

Check your bank balance. Assign every dollar a specific purpose. Question to ask each dollar: *"What do I need this money to do before I get paid again?"*

### Priority hierarchy (the "Priority Cheat Sheet" from Ch 2)
1. **Immediate obligations**, roof, food, electricity, heat. Bad-things-happen-if-skipped.
2. **True expenses** (Rule Two), large/irregular bills you'll face later.
3. **Highest priorities**, the things that make life worth living.
4. **Just for fun**, sky won't fall if these aren't funded a particular month.

### Challenge every assumption (Ch 2)
- Separate honest *obligations* from *habits disguised as necessities*. Cell phone bill is an obligation; the upgrade tier is a habit.
- "You can almost always do something to shrink your spending", even mortgage/rent.
- **Stress as barometer:** if stress is too high, scale back. If you *never* spend to feeling deprived, that's also unsustainable.

### The annual "Question Everything" exercise (Ch 2)
Once a year (Mecham does January), question every line item. Ask **why** 5-7 times to peel back what the spending really represents. (Mecham's example: friend's family loved movie nights → ultimately about family time → switched to home movies, saved $80/night, increased family time.)

### Periodic Fresh Start (Ch 2, Ch 9)
File the old budget. Open a new one with just your bank balance. Add expenses one by one, each must justify its place. Surfaces lifestyle creep.

### Credit Card Rule (Ch 2)
Only charge what's already in your bank account, budgeted for that expense.

**The Credit Card Float trap:** Even if you pay your statement in full each month, if you couldn't pay your CC down to **$0 today**, you're using next month's income to fund this month's spending. To exit: pay down balance + only charge currently-budgeted dollars.

### Lifestyle Creep / "Priority Seep" (Ch 2)
After a raise, money silently flows to expanded comforts. Combat with the annual Question Everything + occasional Fresh Start. Goal isn't austerity, it's intentionality.

## Rule Two: Embrace Your True Expenses (Ch 3)

True expenses = ALL your expenses, daily, monthly, AND irregular. Most budgets fail by ignoring the third category.

### Two camps of irregular expenses
- **Predictable**, known when, known amount (or close): car insurance premiums, holiday shopping, summer AC bills, holiday travel, school tuition. Plan ahead, divide by months until due, fund monthly.
- **Unpredictable but inevitable**, known they'll happen, just not when/how much: car repairs, vet bills, medical, charitable giving impulses, unexpected wedding gifts. Mecham: *"Most surprises aren't surprises"* if you take a broader view.

### How to surface them (Ch 3)
Look at the last 3-6 months of credit card statements. Patterns appear: vet visits, charitable giving, infrequent bills.

### "Rule One Redux" (Ch 3)
Rule Two is just Rule One applied to less frequent expenses. Once true expenses are identified, treat each as a Rule One job and assign monthly dollars.

### Mountain → Hills metaphor
A big lump sum feels paralyzing. The same number in monthly installments is doable. Break every big number down that way and the wall becomes a staircase.

### "Dream Big, Act Small"
Rule Two also funds dreams. Cabin you can't afford yet? Add it as a budget category with $0, the placeholder makes it real and ready to receive dollars when other priorities free up.

### Rule Two Nirvana: No More Emergency Fund
Generic emergency funds get raided because their purpose is vague. Replace with **targeted true-expense reserves** + an **income replacement fund** (call it what it is, money to cover X months of living expenses if income vanishes). Each dollar has a job; nothing sits in "emergency" purgatory.

### Variable income (Ch 3)
Counter-intuitively, variable-income earners need budgets *more* than salaried ones. Smooths the seesaw between panic months and euphoria months. Budget monthly even if you're paid weekly/quarterly/erratically.

## Rule Three: Roll with the Punches (Ch 4)

> *Changing my budget is not failing.*, Ch 4 mantra

Your budget reflects life. Life changes. Budget changes. If you don't allow change, you'll quit.

### Accountability redefined (Ch 4)
You're not accountable to every line item. You're accountable to the **bottom line** (in vs. out). If you overspend on groceries, pull from clothing or restaurants, that's reprioritization, not failure.

### Be honest about patterns (Ch 4)
If you constantly overspend in one area, that line was wishful thinking. Mecham + his wife overspent on groceries for **10 years** before admitting their grocery target was set when they were broke newlyweds, they had to update the number, not their behavior.

### Values vs. priorities (Ch 4)
Priorities can shift quickly. Values are resilient. When a punch lands (sister needs to crash; laptop dies and you need it for work), priorities reshuffle but underlying values (family, security) stay constant. The budget shifts to honor the values.

### Rule Three is just Rule One, all month (Ch 4)
Same exercise on day 1, day 15, day 27, each time, look at remaining dollars and assign jobs against current reality.

## Rule Four: Age Your Money (Ch 5)

Money's "age" = days between when you **earned** it and when you **spend** it. Goal: **30+ days**.

### Why age matters
- Living paycheck-to-paycheck = age of 0 days (or negative, if in debt).
- 30+ days old = a buffer. Surprise bill or income hiccup can't immediately threaten you.
- The relief from Rule Four lasts in a way Rules 1-3 don't on their own.

### Cereal silo metaphor
A grain silo: new grain on top, oldest at bottom. If you take less than what's added each day, the buffer grows. Your bank account is the silo.

### Without Rule Four
Stack of bills waiting for money.

### With Rule Four
Stack of money waiting for bills.

### Path 1: Byproduct of Rules 1-3
Aging is mostly a byproduct of Rules 1-3 done well:
- Rule One forces awareness → less waste.
- Rule Two builds reserves that sit and age.
- Rule Three keeps you in the game long enough to age.

### Path 2: The Sprint
Short, intense period (weeks) where you simultaneously:
- Get a second job / freelance / odd jobs
- Sell things
- Cut spending to bare survival
- Outsource your stuff (Airbnb spare room, rent your van)

Goal: bank one full month of expenses. Use that to budget the *following* month, now your money is 30 days old. Sprints are not sustainable; that's the point.

### Windfalls (Ch 5)
Tax returns, three-paycheck months, bonuses, employer wellness rewards. **Default move: budget windfalls to *next month*, not this month's wants.** Each windfall ages your money.

## How the Rules Compound

Mecham repeatedly admits: *each rule is just Rule One applied to a different scenario.*
- Rule Two = Rule One for less frequent expenses.
- Rule Three = Rule One, redone all month long as life unfolds.
- Rule Four = the byproduct of doing Rules 1-3 long enough.

The rules aren't a sequence to complete, they're a single discipline (intentional dollar-by-dollar allocation) viewed from four angles.

## Couples-Specific Mechanics (Ch 6)

Ch 6 includes the YNAB-recommended Yours/Mine/Ours priority split. Some households use it; others run **one shared pot, no partition.** Whichever you choose, the remaining Ch 6 mechanics apply either way:

### Get to know your money partner (Ch 6)
Three things to learn about each other:
1. **Habits**, daily money behaviors (auto-save vs. save-leftovers; bargain-hunter vs. brand-loyal; pay-in-full vs. pay-minimum)
2. **Ideas**, big-picture views (8 months reserves = peace vs. anxiety? Debt = tool vs. evil?)
3. **What you each bring**, debts, savings, history.

### Monthly Budget Date (Ch 6)
Sit down together once a month. Treat it as a date, not a meeting. Calm setting. The budget is a neutral third party, conversation isn't *your spending vs. my spending*, it's *us vs. the budget*.

### Make it ongoing, not just monthly (Ch 6)
Daily/weekly budget-aware moments make the monthly date easier. *"I could go for sushi tonight, but I'd rather put that money toward our trip next month"* in passing keeps both partners in the loop.

### One shared pile of money (Ch 6)
YNAB recommends joint accounts.

### Per Mecham: priorities can change month-to-month
A partner might have been excited about a trip in January, then by March prefer killing debt. Make the monthly date safe enough that this gets surfaced.

### If you choose one shared pot
- **You give up the per-partner "fun money" stash.** Mecham recommends each partner gets a small unaccountable category. Dropping it is a real trade: you lose a documented pressure valve, and you gain a budget with no unexamined corners. If you drop it, add a small shared discretionary line instead so the budget does not become airless, which is pitfall #1 in Ch 9.
- **Every category is a joint decision.** There is no Yours/Mine/Ours partition to fall back on.

## Debt (Ch 7): "Debt Is Not an Option"

Mecham's only prescriptive chapter. He's even-keeled the rest of the book; on debt, he's loud.

### Why debt matters (his real argument)
Most gurus focus on interest cost. Mecham argues the real damage is **restricted cash flow**, debt claims dollars before they hit your account, so you can't aim them at today's priorities.

### Debt classification
- **Bad debt always:** consumer debt (credit cards), car loans (asset depreciates), anything for things you didn't really care about.
- **Less-bad debt:** mortgage (asset doesn't typically depreciate), structured to fit comfortably in your budget.
- **Education debt:** Mecham is anti-student-loan. He plans to teach his 6 kids to fund college through scholarships, work, and budgeting.

### Mecham's mortgage rule
He pays his mortgages off at "fever pace." Not for interest savings, because he loves living in a paid-for house. Pure preference. Says don't follow him; decide for yourself based on what you value.

### Critical sequencing: True Expenses BEFORE aggressive debt paydown (Ch 7)
> *"As much as I despise debt, I'm actually not telling you to jump right into crushing it."*

Fund Rule Two true expenses FIRST. If you aggressively pay debt while ignoring true expenses, the next surprise bill (car repair, holiday gifts, broken water heater) puts you straight back into credit card debt. The cycle never breaks.

**Sequence:**
1. Build a small buffer (Rule 4 starter, even ~$1k)
2. Fund true expenses (Rule 2)
3. Attack debt aggressively
4. Continue Rule 2 forever (so you never re-enter debt)

### Multiple debts: lowest balance first
Most "debt snowball" advice agrees. Mecham endorses it for **simplicity, not interest math**. Fewer monthly payments = fewer obligations to juggle = more clarity.

### Don't auto-roll freed-up cash flow into the next debt
When one debt is paid off, the cash flow that was going to it is now free. Standard advice: roll it 100% into the next debt. Mecham's twist: *first ask whether there's a higher-priority Rule 2 item that needs that cash flow now.* You're in control; don't let the debt-snowball algorithm decide for you.

### Income matters as much as expense cuts (Ch 7)
Mitchel's story: aggressive debt paydown + cutting expenses got him only so far. His real acceleration came when he raised his income. Don't let lifestyle creep absorb the raise, keep spending the same and dump every extra dollar on the goal.

## Teaching Kids (Ch 8)

Apply when relevant.

- Mecham starts kids on YNAB at age 8. Younger kids get a flat allowance and free use.
- **Allowance separate from chores** (per Ron Lieber's *The Opposite of Spoiled*). Chores are a duty; allowance is a learning tool. Don't yank allowance for unrelated behavior.
- Mecham's rules for kids' budgets: **10% giving + 50% of remainder to savings; rest is theirs.**
- **Start early, go slowly, stay within their reality.** A middle-school kid won't care about prioritizing rent; will care about prioritizing iPod savings. Pick goals at their level.
- Rule One question for kids: *What do I want first?* Let them pick a single category, that's fine. They'll quickly learn money is finite.
- **Letting them spend on regrettable things** (the LeapPad story) is essential. The lesson lands harder when they self-discover the regret.
- Once kids work, they take on more expenses gradually.

## When You Feel Like Quitting (Ch 9)

You will want to quit. This chapter is the recovery playbook.

### The Six Pitfalls (each is self-inflicted perfection)
1. **No breathing room.** Mecham's "doughnut incident", almost quit over a $0.50 doughnut. Solution: budget tiny amounts ($5/month) for fun, *with no questions asked*. Even $5 of "do whatever" money fixes the breathing problem.
2. **Unrealistic spending targets.** Without data, a spending target may be a fantasy. Track first; set targets from real numbers.
3. **Assuming rapid change.** Behavior change takes time. Don't expect to halve grocery spending in 30 days.
4. **Demanding too much of yourself.** Don't obsess. Don't check 5x/day. Don't talk about it constantly. Treat budget like a habit you integrate, not a fad you sprint.
5. **OCD on small transactions.** Sometimes a transaction won't reconcile by a few cents. Let it go. Send a multi-purpose receipt to one category and move on.
6. **Complexity.** Too many bank accounts + too many cards = mental tax. Simplify.

### The Budget Detox (Ch 9)
When the budget feels broken, do a Budget Detox, Mecham's full restart. The Phil/Alexis story is the canonical example:
1. **Get ruthless on each line item.** Question every single one. Cut/optimize where possible.
2. **Phil/Alexis examples:**
   - Natural gas: bill creep over the year. Lowered thermostat + timer → $50/mo saved.
   - Cell phones: switched providers (MrMoneyMustache rec) → $99/mo saved.
   - Karate: kid stopped enjoying. Cancel.
   - Cable: negotiated retention discount → $52/mo saved.
   - Groceries: actually had to *raise* because the old number was wishful.
   - Disney trip: $4k saved for a goal that wasn't even theirs (kid didn't care). Reallocate.
3. **Combine with earning more.** Phil/Alexis paired the detox with launching new income (Alexis subcontracted to mentees, opened up capacity).
4. **Redo the whole budget.** Fresh start, every line justified.

### The Reset Question (Ch 9, ties back to Intro)
When confused or stuck: *Wipe out the whole budget. Forget every obligation. With nothing but a checking account balance: what do I want my money to do for me?* Then rebuild Rule One from scratch.

## What's NOT in this framework (intentionally)

- **Percentage prescriptions** (housing X%, food Y%, retirement Z%). Mecham explicitly rejects these, geography and life choices break them.
- **Investment advice.** Mecham doesn't tell you what to invest in.
- **College savings prescription.** Mecham doesn't save for his 6 kids' college and explains why (Ch 5, 7).
- **Generic emergency fund target** (3 months, 6 months, etc.). Replaced by targeted true-expense reserves (Ch 3).

## How the skills use this file

- **`ynab-coach` Mode 1 (Diagnose)** scores the four rules in order and reports a verdict
  per rule. The thresholds come from this file.
- **Mode 3 (Suggest Budget)** and **Mode 8 Gate C** group categories by the Ch 2 priority
  hierarchy: immediate obligations, true expenses, highest priorities, just for fun. That
  ordering is what makes a cut list defensible.
- **Mode 4 (Budget Detox)** is Ch 9 end to end, opening with the Reset Question.
- **Mode 5 (Decision Filter)** is the Ch 1 and Ch 3 reframe: not "can we afford it" but
  "does this move us closer", with the pushed-back priority named.
- **Mode 6 (Budget Date Prep)** is the Ch 6 monthly date.
- **Mode 7 (Windfall Ritual)** is the Ch 5 default: windfalls go to next month.
- **Rule Zero (Ch 6)** is the gate on Mode 8. Without named priorities an auto-built
  budget is just last quarter's spending with rounder numbers.
